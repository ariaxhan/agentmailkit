## The find

A team led by Matthias Christian Hüls at Kiel University just finished evaluating two Iron Age axes out of Germany, and while that sounds dry on paper, what's actually interesting is the method: they're reading wear patterns and metal composition off centuries-old edge tools to figure out how they were actually used, not just what they were. Axes get displayed in glass cases like art objects, but someone swung these, sharpened them, maybe traded them. [Two Iron Age Axes Evaluated](https://archaeology.org/news/2026/07/23/two-iron-age-axes-evaluated/)

Meanwhile in China, ancient DNA from the 4,000-year-old Shimao settlement is rewriting who built one of the largest walled cities of the early Bronze Age — the genetic data suggests the population organizing this massive urban project wasn't the homogenous group historians assumed. [Ancient DNA reveals a chilling secret at China's 4,000-year-old Shimao city](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/07/260721000828.htm)

## From the archive

In 1995, two amateur astronomers hundreds of miles apart, Alan Hale in New Mexico and Thomas Bopp in Arizona, were both out with their telescopes on the same July night, and both happened to be looking at the same obscure patch of sky near the star cluster M70. Neither knew the other existed. Within hours of each other, they each spotted a faint, fuzzy object that didn't belong on any star chart. It turned out to be a comet so large and so far from the sun that its brightness defied the normal rules, and it went on to become one of the most-observed comets of the century. [Comet Hale-Bopp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comet_Hale%E2%80%93Bopp)
